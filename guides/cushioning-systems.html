Cushioning versus void fill: what protects what

Understand impact absorption, blocking and bracing, and why loose packing paper cannot solve every problem.

Crumpled paper, molded fiber, foam and air pillows are often all called packing material, but they perform different jobs. Distinguishing cushion from restraint is the difference between a parcel that merely looks full and one that keeps a product in place.

ParcelProof EditorialReviewed 11 Nov 2026Independent educational guide

Identify the failure you are preventing

First ask what a foreseeable impact could damage: a corner, a screen, a thin handle, a lid or a surface finish. Then ask whether the object will shift during sorting or transport. Different failure modes need different combinations of materials.

A rigid foam corner can spread loading away from a delicate edge. Loose paper can reduce rattling in gaps. A soft air pillow may help with light nonfragile goods but can collapse or puncture in the wrong application.

Build a protected inner unit

Wrap or brace fragile objects individually, especially when packing two items together. Items touching each other can transmit impact directly even if their combined parcel is surrounded by packing paper.

Where possible, make an inner unit that you can pick up without loose parts falling out. Folded corrugated inserts, dividers, fitted foam or molded supports can turn many loose pieces into one stable assembly.

Separate impact cushion from empty-space fill

Impact cushioning must slow contact with the outside carton. Void fill primarily prevents movement after the protective system is installed. Never assume a bag of loose filler on top will replace side or bottom protection.

Check all six directions. A product resting directly on the box floor has no lower impact buffer. Fill volume around the protected product, but avoid crushing delicate objects with excessive force.

Match material to weight and sensitivity

Heavier objects can compress lightweight padding quickly. Electronics may require antistatic or appropriate protective materials instead of ordinary loose foam. Surface-sensitive finishes may need a nonabrasive protective layer before tougher blocking.

If the item is especially delicate, valuable or difficult to replace, carrier-specific packaged-goods guidance or professional packaging may be the better choice. A single universal cushion thickness does not exist for every product and route.

Use a repeatable movement test

Before final tape, close the package and gently rotate it while supported. Listen for impacts or sliding, not just a small rustle of paper. Open and inspect the relative position if uncertain.

A parcel that passes a gentle movement check can still fail during real handling. Treat the check as a useful screening step, never as a drop-test certification or damage guarantee.

FIELD EXAMPLE / 02

Three ceramic cups in one large carton

A mound of crumpled paper around three unwrapped cups leaves handles and rims vulnerable to direct contact. Wrapping each cup, inserting partitions, cushioning the group on every side and then filling remaining gaps creates a more controlled system.

Decision checkpoint: Select materials based on impact risk, movement control, weight and finish sensitivity; do not judge by how full the carton looks.

Before you seal the box

  • Protect each fragile item separately.
  • Cushion the bottom, sides and top.
  • Keep delicate items from striking each other.
  • Block large gaps without crushing the item.
  • Perform a gentle movement check.

Common questions

Is bubble wrap alone enough?

Not always. A wrapped item may still shift inside an oversized box or be vulnerable to a sharp corner impact.

Should I fill the entire carton tightly?

Fill movement-causing voids without compressing goods or damaging the box. More filler is not always better.
