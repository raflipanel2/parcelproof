Field guide 06 / Materials

Packaging clothing and soft goods without bulk or moisture

Keep fabric clean, prevent snagging, and avoid choosing a bag that can tear under ordinary handling.

Clothing is usually less fragile than glass or electronics, but soft goods can still get wet, snag, soil or fall out of a poorly sealed package. Packing well means balancing protection with unnecessary volume.

Decide between bag and box based on the item

A durable T-shirt may suit an appropriately strong mailer, while a structured hat, leather item or stiff-backed garment may need a box to prevent distortion. Avoid treating every textile as crush-tolerant.

Consider the edges of zippers, buckles and metal accessories. Those details can puncture thin mailers or abrade neighboring fabric if left exposed.

Prepare the garment before closing it

Make sure the item is dry and clean. Fold it neatly with hard accessories turned inward or separately cushioned. Use tissue or another clean layer when surface finish, lint transfer or color bleeding are concerns.

A plastic inner sleeve can offer extra protection from light moisture, but do not trap a damp garment. Freshly cleaned fabrics should be fully dry before sealing for long transport.

Remove damaging excess air without overcompression

Reducing air can keep parcels compact, but extreme compression can crease structured garments or deform padding. A garment that expands after packing can cause bulging seams and rate measurement problems.

Use sensible folding rather than automatically vacuum-packing everything. Test the filled mailer to make sure the contents cannot force open the closure.

Protect against sharp contact and tears

Inspect bag seams and adhesive strips. If a mailer stretches thin around a buckle or box edge, choose another material. No single thin sleeve protects against all handling abuse.

For a returned jacket or pair of shoes, protect soles, hardware and fabric separately. Shoes are often better supported in a sturdy box than in a soft envelope.

Check size and service fit

A very large but light pillow or jacket may be rated by volume with some services. Measure the final sealed outer parcel after the soft contents have settled.

Confirm that the carrier accepts a non-boxed package for your service. Keep return labels away from wrinkles or seams that can interfere with scanning.

FIELD EXAMPLE / 06

A wool cardigan with metal buttons

The cardigan is soft but its buttons can abrade the knit or the mailer. Fold the garment with a thin clean layer over the button line, use a protective inner sleeve and choose a bag that closes flat without pressure on the closure.

Decision checkpoint: Minimize excess space without sacrificing shape or basic protection from moisture and sharp edges.

Before you seal the box

  • Confirm garments are completely dry.
  • Isolate buckles and rough accessories.
  • Choose a mailer or box that resists tearing.
  • Close without visible bulging.
  • Measure the finished parcel.

Common questions

Should clothing always go in a poly mailer?

No. Structured, expensive or accessory-heavy items may need rigid protection.

Can vacuum packing lower shipping cost?

It can reduce volume in some cases, but may harm garment shape and does not change every carrier’s billing method.
